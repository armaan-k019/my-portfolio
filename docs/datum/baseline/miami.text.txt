Datum

April 2026 · Personal Project · Next.js · Leaflet · Claude

Datum is a site analysis tool for architects and urban designers. Enter a US address, set a study radius, and get a dashboard of demographics, transit, amenities, environmental risk, and design implications for that site.

The problem it addresses is time. Early design decisions depend on site context that lives in a dozen places. Median income and population are in the Census. Transit stops and amenities are in OpenStreetMap. Flood zones belong to FEMA. Heat exposure is a weather question. Each source has its own query pattern, and gathering them by hand is slow enough that it often does not happen before the first sketch. Datum pulls them into a single request triggered by an address.

The front end is Next.js with a Leaflet map on OpenStreetMap tiles and a radius control from half a mile to fifty miles, or the kilometer equivalent. Address lookup runs through OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim. Server side API routes then query the Overpass API for amenities and transit, the Census Bureau’s ACS five year estimates for median household income and population (tables B19013 and B01003, located through the Census geocoder), FEMA’s flood hazard layer, and Open-Meteo for temperature. Claude writes a three part design implications section from the result: a summary, an interpretation of each data point, and specific recommendations. Every API key stays on the server.

Most of the work went into the things that fail. The map started on Google Maps and moved to Leaflet mid build after API key domain restrictions and cost made production unreliable. Vercel’s serverless functions returned 504s once five external calls ran in sequence, so the orchestration route now runs with a sixty second limit and the calls run in parallel. Any of those five sources can still time out, which meant rewriting the Claude prompt to reason from whatever data actually arrived rather than assuming a complete set.

Built in April 2026 as a personal project at the intersection of computer science and architecture. Today the free data backends (Overpass, Census, FEMA, Open-Meteo) return live results. Everything else works end to end, and the tool is below.

AI DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

Generated by Claude · for design research only

AI

This site sits at the intersection of Biscayne Boulevard and NE 25th Street in Edgewater, one of Miami's most rapidly transforming waterfront neighborhoods. The location places it squarely between the cultural anchors of Wynwood to the west and Midtown Miami to the north, in a corridor seeing intense residential and mixed-use development pressure.

COMMUNITY PROFILE

Edgewater has evolved from a quiet residential enclave into a dense, young, and internationally diverse urban neighborhood. The area attracts a mix of Latin American transplants, young professionals, and long-term Miami residents who have watched the neighborhood transform around them. It sits at a socioeconomic crossroads, with luxury high-rises now neighbors to older affordable rental stock, meaning any new project here will interact with a genuinely varied income spectrum.

CULTURAL CONTEXT

Edgewater occupies one of Miami's most storied stretches of Biscayne Boulevard, a corridor that once housed the city's mid-century motel and entertainment strip before decades of disinvestment. The neighborhood borders Wynwood, whose art district explosion post-2010 sent ripple effects north, accelerating gentrification along the boulevard. Margaret Pace Park at the waterfront is a genuine community anchor — a rare green gathering space in a concrete-heavy district — and any design here should take cues from that model of activated public waterfront space. Biscayne Boulevard itself still feels like a car-first arterial, but city planning efforts and new development are pushing toward a more pedestrian-friendly identity. The cultural mix is deeply Caribbean and Latin American, with Venezuelan, Colombian, and Brazilian communities particularly well represented.

DATA INSIGHTS

Current Temperature

At 82.2°F / 27.9°C — and that's before accounting for Miami's humidity — passive cooling and shading are non-negotiable design priorities. Brise-soleil, deep overhangs, cross-ventilation, and reflective or green roofing are essential, not optional.

Walkability Score

A score of 0 out of 100 almost certainly reflects a data gap rather than true conditions on the ground — Edgewater is a walkable urban neighborhood by Miami standards. That said, treat it as a prompt: the pedestrian environment along Biscayne Boulevard is hostile enough that your project has a real opportunity to improve the public realm at grade.

Bike Friendliness Score

The 0 out of 100 bike score underscores that cycling infrastructure along this corridor is genuinely underdeveloped despite Miami's flat terrain. Secure, covered bicycle parking and connections to the existing Baywalk and Brickell CitiBike network should be baked into the program.

DESIGN RECOMMENDATIONS

1

Activate the Ground Floor Aggressively

Biscayne Boulevard at this block still reads as a blank wall and parking podium corridor. Pull retail, food and beverage, or community uses to the sidewalk edge with full-height glazing and direct street access. Even a single active corner at NE 25th can begin to stitch together the pedestrian experience between Wynwood and Midtown.

2

Design for Heat as the Primary Climate Driver

With ambient temps in the low 80s as a baseline and Miami's notorious humidity pushing real-feel temperatures significantly higher, every facade orientation matters. Prioritize deep shade on east and west exposures, specify high-performance glazing, and consider semi-outdoor covered terraces as a building amenity — Miamians live outside year-round when shade is available.

3

Incorporate Flood and Resilience Thinking from Day One

Edgewater sits at very low elevation and has experienced nuisance flooding during king tides and heavy rain events. Elevate the ground floor habitable program above base flood elevation with a meaningful buffer, and design parking and mechanical systems accordingly. This is both a code reality and a long-term asset protection strategy.

4

Build in Bike and Micro-Mobility Infrastructure

Despite the low score, Edgewater's flat terrain and proximity to the Baywalk make cycling viable for many residents. Include a well-located, covered bike room with repair station, and design a safe connection from the building's main entry to the street bike lane — or advocate for a protected lane segment as part of the project's public benefit package.

5

Plan a Unit Mix for the Actual Rental Market Here

Edgewater skews toward younger renters and smaller households, but also has a growing population of families drawn by relative affordability compared to Brickell. A mix weighted toward one- and two-bedrooms with a handful of three-bedroom units, plus flex work-from-home space within units, reflects how people are actually living here post-pandemic.

6

Reference Waterfront and Tropical Landscape in Design Language

Proximity to Biscayne Bay and Margaret Pace Park gives this neighborhood a strong natural identity that good architecture should amplify rather than ignore. Native and climate-adapted planting on terraces and at grade, water features that double as stormwater management, and a material palette that references the bay's blues and greens can root the project meaningfully in its place.
