Datum

April 2026 · Personal Project · Next.js · Leaflet · Claude

Datum is a site analysis tool for architects and urban designers. Enter a US address, set a study radius, and get a dashboard of demographics, transit, amenities, environmental risk, and design implications for that site.

The problem it addresses is time. Early design decisions depend on site context that lives in a dozen places. Median income and population are in the Census. Transit stops and amenities are in OpenStreetMap. Flood zones belong to FEMA. Heat exposure is a weather question. Each source has its own query pattern, and gathering them by hand is slow enough that it often does not happen before the first sketch. Datum pulls them into a single request triggered by an address.

The front end is Next.js with a Leaflet map on OpenStreetMap tiles and a radius control from half a mile to fifty miles, or the kilometer equivalent. Address lookup runs through OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim. Server side API routes then query the Overpass API for amenities and transit, the Census Bureau’s ACS five year estimates for median household income and population (tables B19013 and B01003, located through the Census geocoder), FEMA’s flood hazard layer, and Open-Meteo for temperature. Claude writes a three part design implications section from the result: a summary, an interpretation of each data point, and specific recommendations. Every API key stays on the server.

Most of the work went into the things that fail. The map started on Google Maps and moved to Leaflet mid build after API key domain restrictions and cost made production unreliable. Vercel’s serverless functions returned 504s once five external calls ran in sequence, so the orchestration route now runs with a sixty second limit and the calls run in parallel. Any of those five sources can still time out, which meant rewriting the Claude prompt to reason from whatever data actually arrived rather than assuming a complete set.

Built in April 2026 as a personal project at the intersection of computer science and architecture. Today the free data backends (Overpass, Census, FEMA, Open-Meteo) return live results. Everything else works end to end, and the tool is below.

WaKeeney is a small rural county seat on the High Plains of western Kansas, sitting along I-70 in Trego County. This is a car-dependent agricultural community where design must respond to wide open spaces, extreme weather, and the self-reliant character of rural Great Plains life.

COMMUNITY PROFILE

WaKeeney has a population of roughly 1,800 people serving as the commercial and civic hub for Trego County's broader agricultural region. The community skews older, as is typical of rural western Kansas towns that have experienced decades of gradual outmigration of young adults. Residents here are deeply rooted, often multigenerational farm families and the tradespeople, healthcare workers, and small business owners who support them. Life is organized around driving, seasonal agricultural rhythms, and strong community institutions like churches, the county fair, and the local school system.

CULTURAL CONTEXT

WaKeeney calls itself the 'Christmas City of the High Plains,' a nod to its beloved holiday light tradition that draws visitors from across the region and speaks to the town's fierce local pride. Sitting at the geographic crossroads of I-70 and US-283, it has historically been a waypoint town — a place where travelers stop, where ranchers resupply, and where the county comes together. The surrounding Smoky Hill River valley and the vast shortgrass prairie define the visual and cultural landscape here; there's a stoic beauty to this place that good architecture should acknowledge rather than ignore. Recent decades have brought the familiar pressures of rural America — consolidation, population loss, and the challenge of keeping Main Street viable — making any new investment genuinely meaningful to the community.

DATA INSIGHTS

Walkability Score

A score of 0 out of 100 confirms this is a fully car-dependent environment. Every program element must assume that every visitor arrives by personal vehicle, so generous, well-organized parking and clear vehicular circulation aren't optional — they're the primary arrival experience.

Bike Friendliness Score

A score of 0 out of 100 means cycling infrastructure is essentially nonexistent. While long-term planning could incorporate a simple trail connection to downtown, bike parking and dedicated lanes should not consume design budget that could be spent on wind and weather protection.

Transit Stops

Zero transit stops within 2 miles means there is no public transportation network to leverage. Design for ADA-accessible drop-off zones and consider the needs of elderly or mobility-limited residents who may rely on family or community volunteer drivers rather than transit.

Parks Nearby

No parks recorded within the study radius suggests an opportunity — even a modest landscaped gathering space or shaded outdoor seating area associated with this project could serve real community need in a place where such amenities are sparse.

Restaurants and Cafes

Zero food and beverage establishments within 2 miles is a significant contextual fact. If the program allows any flexibility, a small café, food truck pad, or at minimum a covered outdoor eating area could become a genuine community asset and activate the site.

Current Temperature

At 78°F today, the climate is pleasant, but western Kansas is known for brutal summer heat pushing past 100°F, bitter winter wind chills, and severe storm exposure. Passive design strategies — deep overhangs, earth-sheltering concepts, and durable low-maintenance materials — are not stylistic choices here, they're necessities.

DESIGN RECOMMENDATIONS

1

Design for the Car as Primary Occupant

With zero walkability and no transit, the parking lot is effectively the front door of your project. Invest in clear wayfinding, quality paving, generous turning radii for trucks and farm vehicles, and covered drop-off for weather protection. A well-designed parking area with native grass islands and shade trees will dramatically improve the first impression of any building here.

2

Build for Extreme Weather Durability

Western Kansas delivers some of the most punishing weather in North America — high winds, hail, blizzards, and searing summer heat all within the same year. Specify metal roofing, fiber cement or masonry cladding, and triple-glazed windows as baseline assumptions. Deep roof overhangs on the south and west will cut cooling loads significantly during those 100°F summer days.

3

Create a Sheltered Outdoor Gathering Space

The absence of parks or public outdoor amenity within 2 miles is a genuine gap this project could help fill. A simple covered pavilion, windbreak planting on the north and west sides, and a few picnic tables oriented to the south could become a beloved community spot. This is especially meaningful in a town where public gathering infrastructure is limited.

4

Anchor to Main Street's Civic Identity

WaKeeney's Main Street is the cultural and commercial heart of a wide rural catchment area — people drive 30 to 40 miles to come here for services. Architecture on South Main should reinforce that civic character with a clear street presence, human-scaled façade, and materials that feel rooted rather than imported. Avoid the generic metal-building aesthetic that undercuts Main Street vitality.

5

Plan for Programmatic Flexibility

Small rural towns need buildings that can adapt as community needs evolve. Design structural bays and utility rough-ins that could accommodate a range of uses over time — what starts as a retail space may need to become a community meeting room or telehealth clinic in ten years. Open floor plates and accessible restrooms from day one make this resilience affordable.

6

Engage Local Contractors and Regional Materials

In a community this size, how a project is built matters as much as what is built. Specifying materials available through regional suppliers and designing details that local construction trades can execute confidently will keep costs realistic and build goodwill. A technically ambitious design that requires out-of-state specialty contractors will strain the budget and may create long-term maintenance headaches.

Datum is a design research tool. Always verify data with official sources before making design decisions.
