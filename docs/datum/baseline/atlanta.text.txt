Datum

April 2026 · Personal Project · Next.js · Leaflet · Claude

Datum is a site analysis tool for architects and urban designers. Enter a US address, set a study radius, and get a dashboard of demographics, transit, amenities, environmental risk, and design implications for that site.

The problem it addresses is time. Early design decisions depend on site context that lives in a dozen places. Median income and population are in the Census. Transit stops and amenities are in OpenStreetMap. Flood zones belong to FEMA. Heat exposure is a weather question. Each source has its own query pattern, and gathering them by hand is slow enough that it often does not happen before the first sketch. Datum pulls them into a single request triggered by an address.

The front end is Next.js with a Leaflet map on OpenStreetMap tiles and a radius control from half a mile to fifty miles, or the kilometer equivalent. Address lookup runs through OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim. Server side API routes then query the Overpass API for amenities and transit, the Census Bureau’s ACS five year estimates for median household income and population (tables B19013 and B01003, located through the Census geocoder), FEMA’s flood hazard layer, and Open-Meteo for temperature. Claude writes a three part design implications section from the result: a summary, an interpretation of each data point, and specific recommendations. Every API key stays on the server.

Most of the work went into the things that fail. The map started on Google Maps and moved to Leaflet mid build after API key domain restrictions and cost made production unreliable. Vercel’s serverless functions returned 504s once five external calls ran in sequence, so the orchestration route now runs with a sixty second limit and the calls run in parallel. Any of those five sources can still time out, which meant rewriting the Claude prompt to reason from whatever data actually arrived rather than assuming a complete set.

Built in April 2026 as a personal project at the intersection of computer science and architecture. Today the free data backends (Overpass, Census, FEMA, Open-Meteo) return live results. Everything else works end to end, and the tool is below.

AI DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

Generated by Claude · for design research only

AI

This site sits at the intersection of Georgia Tech's campus edge and the historically significant Old Fourth Ward in Atlanta — a zone of intense urban transformation where institutional, residential, and commercial forces are actively reshaping the neighborhood. The location on Techwood Drive places it squarely in one of Atlanta's most storied corridors, requiring design that acknowledges both the university's gravitational pull and the neighborhood's deep community roots.

COMMUNITY PROFILE

The immediate area draws a notably mixed population: Georgia Tech students and faculty, long-term residents of the Old Fourth Ward, and a growing wave of young professionals attracted by the BeltLine and Ponce City Market nearby. The broader Midtown-Old Fourth Ward axis is one of Atlanta's fastest-growing submarkets, meaning the community is in active flux. Any project here will serve people at very different life stages — from 20-year-old undergrads to families who've lived in the ward for decades — and the design should hold space for that diversity.

CULTURAL CONTEXT

Techwood Drive carries enormous historical weight: Techwood Homes, built here in 1936, was the first public housing project in the United States. Its demolition ahead of the 1996 Atlanta Olympics and replacement with Centennial Place was a pivotal — and contested — moment in Atlanta's urban history, displacing thousands of low-income residents. The Old Fourth Ward itself is the birthplace of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., and that legacy of civil rights and community resilience runs deep in the neighborhood's identity. Today the area is a flashpoint for Atlanta's gentrification debate, with the BeltLine's Eastside Trail driving significant displacement pressure from the east. Any project on this site carries a responsibility to engage honestly with that history rather than paper over it.

DATA INSIGHTS

Walkability Score

A score of 0/100 almost certainly reflects a data gap rather than true conditions — Techwood Drive near Georgia Tech is surrounded by urban infrastructure. That said, treat this as a prompt to conduct your own pedestrian audit: sidewalk continuity, crossing safety, and shade coverage on a 91°F day are all genuinely critical issues to assess on the ground.

Current Temperature

At 91.8°F / 33.2°C, Atlanta's summer heat is a dominant design force. Thermal comfort in outdoor spaces, passive cooling strategies, and tree canopy are not amenities here — they are necessities, particularly for a site on the edge of a heat-island-prone urban campus.

Bike Friendliness Score

The 0/100 score warrants field verification, especially given proximity to the BeltLine network and Georgia Tech's active cycling culture. If bike infrastructure is genuinely weak near the site, a strong bicycle parking and storage program — with direct connectivity to the BeltLine — would be a high-value investment.

DESIGN RECOMMENDATIONS

1

Prioritize Shade and Passive Cooling

With temperatures regularly hitting the low 90s, any outdoor program — plazas, entries, waiting areas, ground-floor retail spill-out — needs aggressive tree canopy and shaded structure. Consider orienting the building to cast afternoon shade on pedestrian edges, and use high-albedo paving to reduce radiant heat at street level.

2

Connect Deliberately to the BeltLine

The Eastside BeltLine Trail is within reach of this site and is the primary active-mobility corridor in this part of Atlanta. Design bike storage, wayfinding, and pedestrian routes with a clear line to that network. This dramatically improves the site's effective walkability and bikability regardless of what the scored data shows.

3

Build in Affordability Anchors

Given Techwood Drive's history as the site of America's first public housing project and the ongoing displacement pressures in Old Fourth Ward, there is both a moral and a civic design argument for incorporating affordable units or community-serving ground-floor uses. Engaging with the City of Atlanta's BeltLine Affordable Housing Trust Fund could provide both financing and community credibility.

4

Design Ground Floor for Community, Not Just Activation

Generic 'activated retail' won't resonate here — the ground floor should reflect the cultural specificity of this corridor. Think about uses that serve both Georgia Tech's community and long-term Old Fourth Ward residents: a neighborhood market, a community meeting room, or locally-owned food and service tenants rather than national chains.

5

Conduct a Real Pedestrian Audit Before Finalizing Site Access

The mobility data appears unreliable for this location, so don't design site access and entry points off the scored data alone. Walk Techwood Drive at multiple times of day, map where people are actually moving, and identify where sidewalk gaps or unsafe crossings exist. Your entry sequence should respond to observed pedestrian flows, not assumed ones.

6

Acknowledge History in the Architecture

This block has an extraordinary story — from the nation's first public housing project to an Olympic transformation to the BeltLine era. A meaningful interpretive gesture, whether through public art, a community room with historical exhibits, or simply a design narrative that's transparent about the site's past, would distinguish this project and build genuine neighborhood trust.
