This study investigates the relationship between indoor environments and the effectiveness of Horticulture Therapy in long-term care settings. Using a mixed-methods approach across two therapy rooms, the research combines behavioral observation, semi-structured interviews, mood assessments, environmental quality measurements, and space syntax analysis. Findings indicate that spatial integration, visual access to outdoor gardens, and noise consistency significantly influence engagement levels, emotional outcomes, and social interaction, with participants in the better-integrated room showing notably higher rates of positive affect and physical activity. The results point toward design implications for therapeutic spaces that support not only physical comfort but the social dynamics that drive meaningful engagement among older adults.

## Abstract

Horticulture Therapy has demonstrated positive effects on emotional well-being and social connection among older adults in long-term care. Due to weather conditions and residents' mobility, many Horticulture Therapy sessions actually happen indoors instead of outdoors. However, the indoor environments where Horticulture Therapy is conducted are rarely evaluated, despite evidence that acoustics, lighting, and spatial layout influence stress and social interaction in dementia care settings. This pilot study addresses this gap by examining the relationships among indoor environmental quality, spatial configuration, and residents' emotional, physical, and social responses during Horticulture Therapy sessions. Using a mixed-methods approach, data were collected from six cognitively capable nursing home residents participating in Horticulture Therapy sessions across two therapy rooms, led by the same therapist. Methods included structured behavioral observations of indoor Horticulture Therapy sessions recorded at five-minute intervals, semi-structured interviews exploring residents' connections to nature and Horticulture Therapy experiences, pre-/post-Horticulture Therapy session mood assessments using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule-Short Form (PANAS-SF), indoor environmental quality measurements, and space syntax analysis of spatial configuration. Thematic analysis was conducted on interviews. Both rooms provided acceptable thermal conditions, but Room A has slightly lower average noise (54.1 dbA) but a wider range of noise levels compared to Room B (55.4 dbA, min: max). Room B is also located in an area with higher integration value and better visibility to outside gardens. Behavioral data revealed an increase in physical activity across all participants, with the greatest gains among individuals initially classified as low engagers, while social interaction increased progressively across sessions. Interviews emphasized companionship, emotional calming, and lifelong ties to gardening as central to the therapy experience, with environmental concerns focusing on noise and the lack of a dedicated therapy space. Participants in Room B have a higher ratio of activities and more high-intensity and moderate-high intensity activities compared to Room A (96.55% vs. 62.5%, and 75.86% vs. 31.25% respectively). During Horticulture Therapy sessions, participants in Room B also showed higher ratio of positive emotions (72.41% vs. 31.25%) and lower ratio of negative emotions (25% vs. 3.45%) than those in Room A. There is also a slightly higher social interaction ratio in Room B than Room A (68.97% vs. 62.5%). It seems that the better visual integration and visual access to outdoor gardens, and more stable and consistent noise range support better Horticulture Therapy experiences. These findings suggest that Horticulture Therapy effectiveness depends on an interconnected system of environmental, social, and individual factors rather than isolated factors. The results point to implications for designing therapeutic spaces that support not only physical comfort but also the social dynamics that drive meaningful engagement among older adults.
